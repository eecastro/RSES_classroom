What this scale is (and isn’t)

Morris Rosenberg published this scale in 1965 after surveying about 5,000 high school students in New York State. It measures global self-esteem: your overall sense of your own worth, rather than how confident you feel about a specific skill like math or sports. It went on to become one of the most widely used self-report measures in psychology.

  • Why flip half the items? Some people tend to agree with statements no matter what they say (called acquiescence bias). Mixing positive and negative wording means a person who just agrees with everything lands near the middle instead of looking artificially high.
  • Reliability. Across many studies the ten items hang together well (internal consistency is usually around .80 to .90), and scores are fairly stable over weeks and months.
  • Validity. Scores relate in expected ways to other measures, such as being associated with life satisfaction and inversely associated with depression and anxiety. A correlation like this doesn’t tell us which causes which.
  • Limits of self-report. Your score depends on honest, accurate self-reflection. Mood on the day, wanting to look good (social desirability), and cultural norms about modesty can all nudge scores.
  • Not a diagnosis. This is a research and teaching tool. A single score says nothing definitive about you, and there are no official clinical cutoffs.

Questions to think about

  • Would your score change if you took this during finals week? What does that suggest about whether self-esteem is a stable trait or a changing state?
  • If someone wanted to look good, which items would be easiest to “fake”? How might a researcher catch that?
  • Rosenberg’s original sample was American teenagers in the early 1960s. What might change if you used this scale in a different culture or age group?
